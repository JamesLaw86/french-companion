Every Tense, Simply

💡 The big picture French tenses come in pairs: a simple form (one word: je mange) and a compound form built with an auxiliary — avoir or être — plus a past participle (j’ai mangé). Once you know the simple tenses, every compound tense is “conjugate the auxiliary in that tense, add the participle”. That one idea unlocks half this page.

Le présent — the present

What it means: what is happening now, what happens in general, and — more than in English — what is about to happen. French has no separate “-ing” form: je mange covers both “I eat” and “I am eating”.

Je travaille à Londres.
I work in London. / I’m working in London.
J’arrive dans deux minutes.
I’ll be there in two minutes. (present used for the near future — very common)

How to build it: regular -er verbs drop the ending and add -e, -es, -e, -ons, -ez, -ent. (-ir: -is, -is, -it, -issons, -issez, -issent; -re: -s, -s, —, -ons, -ez, -ent.)

🪄 Trick: “être en train de” If you really want to stress that something is happening right now, use être en train de + infinitive: Je suis en train de manger — “I’m (in the middle of) eating.”

Le passé composé — the everyday past

What it means: a completed action. “I did it, it happened, it’s done.” This is the past tense of conversation — if you’re telling someone what happened yesterday, you live in the passé composé.

How to build it: present tense of avoir (or être for a small club of verbs) + past participle.

J’ai mangé une crêpe.
I ate a crêpe. / I have eaten a crêpe. (French doesn’t distinguish these)
Elle est partie à midi.
She left at noon. (partir takes être — note the participle agrees: partie)

Which verbs take être? Reflexive verbs (se lever, se souvenir…) plus roughly the “coming and going” verbs: aller, venir, arriver, partir, entrer, sortir, monter, descendre, rester, tomber, naître, mourir, retourner, passer, devenir, revenir. With être, the participle agrees with the subject (elles sont arrivées).

Past participles: -er → -é (mangé), -ir → -i (fini), -re → -u (vendu). The important irregulars are worth just memorising:

L’imparfait — the background past

What it means: the past as scenery. Descriptions, habits, ongoing states — anything where you can’t point at the moment it finished. English equivalents: “was doing”, “used to do”, “would (habitually) do”, or plain “was/were”.

Quand j’étais petit, on allait en Bretagne chaque été.
When I was little, we used to go to Brittany every summer. (habit)
Il pleuvait et j’étais fatigué.
It was raining and I was tired. (description / state)

How to build it: take the nous form of the present, drop -ons, add -ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient. This works for every verb in French except être (stem ét-: j’étais).

nous finissons → je finissais · nous faisons → je faisais · nous avons → j’avais
One rule, one exception (être). Genuinely the friendliest tense in the language.

Passé composé vs imparfait — the classic confusion

Both are “the past”, so which one? Think of telling a story as filming a scene:

  • Imparfait = the camera panning over the scene. What things were like, what was going on.
  • Passé composé = the events. The things that happened, one after another, moving the story forward.
Je marchais dans la rue quand j’ai vu un accident.
I was walking down the street (background) when I saw an accident (event).
Il faisait beau, alors on a décidé de sortir.
The weather was nice (scenery), so we decided to go out (event).
🪄 Trick: the interruption test If you could naturally add “…when suddenly something happened”, it’s imparfait. If it is the “something happened”, it’s passé composé. Also: “used to …” or “was …-ing” in English → almost always imparfait.
⚠️ Watch out Some verbs change flavour between the two: je savais = I knew (state) but j’ai su = I found out; je voulais = I wanted, but j’ai voulu = I tried / decided to; je pouvais = I was able (in general), j’ai pu = I managed to.

Le plus-que-parfait — the past before the past

What it means: exactly English “had done”. You’re already talking about the past, and you need to reach back to something even earlier.

How to build it: imparfait of avoir/être + past participle. (Compound-tense rule in action: same recipe as passé composé, just move the auxiliary back a tense.)

Quand je suis arrivé, elle était déjà partie.
When I arrived (past), she had already left (before that).
Je ne savais pas que tu avais vécu en France.
I didn’t know you had lived in France.
💡 Rule of thumb If you’d say “had + past participle” in English, use plus-que-parfait. The match is nearly one-to-one, which makes this an easy tense that merely looks scary.

Le futur proche — “going to”

What it means: “I’m going to do X.” Plans, intentions, things about to happen. In spoken French this has largely taken over from the futur simple.

How to build it: present of aller + infinitive. That’s it.

Je vais appeler ma mère ce soir.
I’m going to call my mum tonight.
Il va pleuvoir.
It’s going to rain.
🪄 Trick: your default future When speaking, make futur proche your default. It’s always understood, never wrong in conversation, and needs zero new conjugation — just aller, which you already know. This is one of the great “lever verbs” — more on the Speaking Tricks page.

Le futur simple — “will”

What it means: “I will do X.” Predictions, promises, more distant or less certain futures, and writing. Feels slightly more formal/considered than futur proche.

How to build it: infinitive + -ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont (the endings are basically avoir!). -re verbs drop the final e: vendre → je vendrai.

Je te rappellerai demain.
I’ll call you back tomorrow.
Quand tu seras grand, tu comprendras.
When you’re older, you’ll understand. (French uses future after quand here — English uses present)

Le futur antérieur — “will have done”

What it means: something that will be finished before another future point. Compound-tense rule again: futur simple of avoir/être + participle.

Quand tu arriveras, j’aurai fini.
By the time you arrive, I’ll have finished.

Low priority for speaking — recognise it, don’t stress about producing it.

Le conditionnel présent — “would”

What it means: “would”. Hypotheticals, politeness, softened statements.

How to build it: future stem + imparfait endings (-ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient). If you know the future and the imparfait, the conditional is free.

Je voudrais un café, s’il vous plaît.
I’d like a coffee, please. (the single most useful conjugated verb in France)
Si j’avais le temps, je voyagerais plus.
If I had the time, I would travel more.
💡 The “si” pattern Si + imparfait, then conditional: Si j’étais riche, j’achèterais une maison. Never put the conditional in the si half — same as English never says “if I would be rich”.

Le conditionnel passé — “would have”

What it means: “would have done” — regrets and might-have-beens. Conditional of avoir/être + participle.

J’aurais aimé venir, mais je travaillais.
I would have liked to come, but I was working.
Si j’avais su, je serais resté chez moi.
If I’d known, I would have stayed home. (si + plus-que-parfait → conditionnel passé)

Le passé simple — recognise, don’t produce

The literary past tense. You’ll meet it in novels and history books (il alla, elle fit, ils furent) but virtually never in speech — spoken French uses the passé composé instead. Learn to recognise the shapes, and move on with your life:

il fut (être) · il eut (avoir) · il alla (aller) · il fit (faire) · il dit (dire) · il vint (venir)
If a verb form in a novel looks alien, it’s probably passé simple. Mentally swap in passé composé.
