Spoken French

The words and phrases you hear constantly in real French — in cafés, on podcasts, in YouTube comments — that courses somehow never teach. Learn these and spoken French suddenly gets much less mysterious.

The big connectors

du coup — “so / as a result / anyway”

The most overused word in modern spoken French (yes, it’s du coup, not de coup). Literally “from the blow”, it means “so / as a consequence”, but in practice it’s a conversation-mover: French speakers use it to link almost any sentence to the previous one.

Il pleuvait, du coup on est restés à la maison.
It was raining, so we stayed home.
Du coup, tu viens ce soir ou pas ?
So… are you coming tonight or not? (here it barely means anything — just “so, anyway”)

quand même — “still / all the same / really though”

The Swiss army knife. Core meaning: “despite that”. But it also adds emphasis, mild indignation, or admiration depending on tone.

C’était cher, mais je l’ai acheté quand même.
It was expensive, but I bought it anyway.
C’est quand même incroyable !
It really is incredible! (emphasis)
Tu aurais pu me prévenir, quand même.
You could have warned me, you know. (mild reproach)

en fait — “actually”

Pronounced with the final t: “on FETT”. Used constantly to correct, explain, or just start a sentence. Not to be confused with au fait (“by the way”).

En fait, je ne suis jamais allé à Paris.
Actually, I’ve never been to Paris.
Au fait, tu as vu Marie hier ?
By the way, did you see Marie yesterday?

bref — “anyway / long story short”

Bref, on a raté le train.
Long story short, we missed the train. (great for ending a rambling story — including your own)

d’ailleurs / en plus — “by the way / what’s more”

J’adore ce resto. D’ailleurs, c’est là qu’on s’est rencontrés.
I love this restaurant. As it happens, that’s where we met.
C’est pas cher, et en plus c’est bon.
It’s cheap, and on top of that it’s good.

Fillers & softeners

ben / bah — “well…”

The French “um/well”. Ben oui = “well, yeah (obviously)”. Ben non = “well, no”. You’ll hear bah ouais a hundred times a day.

— Tu viens ? — Ben oui, évidemment !
— You coming? — Well yeah, obviously!

quoi — end-of-sentence “y’know”

Tacked onto the end of sentences for emphasis, meaning roughly “y’know / basically / that’s all”. Completely untranslatable, completely ubiquitous.

C’est compliqué, quoi.
It’s complicated, y’know.

genre — “like”

The French teenager’s (and increasingly everyone’s) “like”. Introduces examples or approximations, or expresses disbelief on its own.

Il y avait genre cinquante personnes.
There were like fifty people.
Genre ! Il t’a vraiment dit ça ?
No way! He really said that to you?

enfin — “well, I mean…”

Literally “finally”, but in speech it’s mostly a self-correction: you say something, then walk it back. Often shortened to ’fin.

Il est sympa… enfin, quand il veut.
He’s nice… well, I mean, when he wants to be.

bon — “right / OK then”

Bon, on y va ?
Right, shall we go? (Bon ben… = “right, well…” — the classic pre-goodbye)

Useful “formal” phrases you’ll actually meet

en tant que — “as / in my capacity as”

En tant que débutant, j’ai le droit de faire des erreurs.
As a beginner, I’m allowed to make mistakes.

quant à — “as for”

Quant à moi, je préfère le vin.
As for me, I prefer wine.

par contre — “on the other hand / however”

Le film était bien. Par contre, c’était trop long.
The film was good. On the other hand, it was too long. (spoken; in writing use en revanche)

du genre / dans le genre — “along the lines of”

Il m’a dit un truc du genre « c’est pas grave ».
He said something like “it doesn’t matter”.

Spoken habits to know

💡 The “ne” disappears In speech, negation drops the ne: Je sais pas (or even j’sais pas, sounding like “shay pa”), c’est pas grave, il y a pas (→ y’a pas). The pas does all the work. You should still write the ne, but don’t be surprised when you never hear it.
💡 “On” replaced “nous” Spoken French almost never says nous allons — it says on va. On conjugates like il/elle (third person singular), so it’s also easier. On y va ? = “Shall we go?”
💡 Words get swallowed Tu as → t’as. Tu es → t’es. Il y a → y’a. Je suis → j’suis (“shwee”). Petit → p’tit. Half the difficulty of listening comprehension is just these five contractions.
💡 C’est + everything Spoken French leans hard on c’est: c’est qui ? (who is it?), c’est où ? (where is it?), c’est quoi ce truc ? (what is this thing?). Textbook question forms like Qu’est-ce que c’est ? exist, but c’est quoi ? is what you’ll hear.
