Core Verbs, Deep Dive

Four verbs — avoir, être, aller, faire — do a huge share of the work in French: as verbs in their own right, as auxiliaries, and inside dozens of idioms. This page is the reference for how they combine, including the confusing double-ups like j’ai eu and j’ai été.

Verbs wearing two hats

The key to every “why is avoir in there twice?” confusion is this: avoir and être each have two jobs.

  • As a meaning verb: avoir = to have, être = to be.
  • As an auxiliary: a grammatical helper that carries the tense in compound forms (j’ai mangé, elle est partie). Here it means nothing by itself — it’s scaffolding, like English “have” in “I have eaten”.

Since the auxiliary can attach to any verb — including avoir and être themselves — you get forms where the same verb appears in both jobs at once. That’s not an exception; it’s the system working normally.

j’ai été, j’avais été, j’ai eu — untangled

Apply the compound-tense recipe (auxiliary + participle) to être and avoir themselves. The participles are été (from être) and eu (from avoir), and both verbs take avoir as their auxiliary:

FormRecipeMeaning
j’ai étépresent of avoir + étéI was / I have been (passé composé of être)
j’avais étéimparfait of avoir + étéI had been (plus-que-parfait of être)
j’ai eupresent of avoir + euI had / I got (passé composé of avoir — avoir helping itself!)
j’avais euimparfait of avoir + euI had had (plus-que-parfait of avoir)
j’aurai été / eufutur of avoir + participleI will have been / had
j’aurais été / euconditionnel of avoir + participleI would have been / had
J’ai été malade la semaine dernière.
I was ill last week. (a bounded episode → passé composé)
J’avais été malade juste avant les vacances, donc j’étais fatigué.
I had been ill just before the holidays, so I was tired. (past before the past)
J’ai eu de la chance.
I was lucky / I got lucky. (literally “I had luck” — avoir is both auxiliary and meaning verb)
🪄 The rule, in one line “Avoir helps everyone — including être and itself.” When you see two avoir-ish words in a row, the first is the auxiliary (carries the tense), the second is a participle (carries the meaning). Read j’avais eu as [j’avais = tense: had] + [eu = meaning: had/got] — exactly like English “I had had”.
⚠️ j’ai été vs j’étais Both translate as “I was”, so which? Same logic as any passé composé vs imparfait choice: j’étais for background states (j’étais fatigué — I was tired), j’ai été for bounded episodes or changes (j’ai été surpris — I was surprised [at that moment]). When in doubt with descriptions, j’étais is usually right.
💡 Bonus: “j’ai été” for “I went” In casual speech you’ll hear j’ai été à Paris meaning “I went to Paris” (standard: je suis allé). Considered incorrect by purists, said by millions daily. Recognise it; imitate it or not, as you like.

Participle: eu (pronounced “ü”, one sound). Auxiliary: avoir.

Where English uses “be”, French often uses avoir. This list is worth over-learning, because getting it wrong (je suis faim) is the classic tell:

avoir faim / soif / chaud / froid / peur / raison / tort / sommeil / … ans
to be hungry / thirsty / hot / cold / afraid / right / wrong / sleepy / … years old
J’ai besoin de café. · J’ai envie de partir. · J’ai hâte !
I need coffee. · I feel like leaving. · I can’t wait!
Il y a un problème. · Il y avait du monde. · Il y aura du soleil.
There’s a problem. · There were lots of people. · There’ll be sunshine. (il y a = avoir in disguise)

Participle: été. Auxiliary: avoir — être never helps itself (j’ai été, never “je suis été”).

Être’s auxiliary club. As an auxiliary, être serves reflexive verbs (je me suis levé) and the movement/change verbs — a common mnemonic is DR & MRS VANDERTRAMP:

Devenir Revenir Monter Rester Sortir Venir Aller Naître Descendre Entrer Retourner Tomber Rentrer Arriver Mourir Partir
Or just think: verbs about coming, going, and changing state. With être, the participle agrees: elle est venue, ils sont partis.
⚠️ Six that switch sides Monter, descendre, sortir, rentrer, retourner, passer take avoir when they have a direct object: Elle est sortie (she went out) but Elle a sorti la poubelle (she took out the bin).

Participle: allé. Auxiliary: être (je suis allé).

Aller’s superpowers:

Je vais te montrer.
I’m going to show you. — aller + infinitive = the futur proche, your default spoken future.
Ça va ? — Ça va. · Ça va aller.
How’s it going? — Fine. · It’ll be OK. (aller also means “to be doing [well/badly]”)
On y va ? · Allez !
Shall we go? · Come on! / Go on! (Allez doubles as all-purpose encouragement)

Participle: fait. Auxiliary: avoir. (Pronunciation quirk: faisons/faisais — the “ai” sounds like “euh”: “feuh-zon”.)

Faire is everywhere: weather, activities, and a mountain of idioms.

Il fait beau / froid / chaud / nuit.
The weather’s nice / it’s cold / hot / dark. (weather belongs to faire, not être)
faire du sport · faire les courses · faire la cuisine · faire attention · faire semblant · faire exprès
to do sport · do the shopping · cook · pay attention · pretend · do it on purpose
Ça fait combien ? · Ça fait deux ans que j’apprends le français.
How much is that? · I’ve been learning French for two years. (ça fait … que = “it’s been … that”)
Je vais faire réparer ma voiture.
I’m going to have my car repaired. (faire + infinitive = to have something done)

Agreement in one minute

  • With être: participle agrees with the subject. Elle est partie. Ils sont arrivés.
  • With avoir: no agreement… unless the direct object comes before the verb: J’ai vu les photos → Je les ai vues.
  • Good news: most of these agreements are silent in speech (parti/partie sound identical). Care about them when writing; don’t let them slow your speaking.
