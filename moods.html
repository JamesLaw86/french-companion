Moods & the Subjunctive

Tenses say when; moods say how real. Once you see that distinction, the subjunctive stops being a monster — and if you still don’t fancy it, there’s a whole section on legally avoiding it.

What a mood is

A tense places an action in time: past, present, future. A mood signals the speaker’s relationship to the action: is it a fact, a command, a hypothesis, or something filtered through wanting, doubting or feeling? Each mood then has its own tenses inside it. Everything on the Tenses page lives inside one mood — the indicative.

The four moods you use

MoodSignalsExample
IndicatifFacts, realityTu viens. — You’re coming.
ImpératifCommandsViens ! — Come!
ConditionnelHypotheticals, politenessTu viendrais ? — Would you come?
SubjonctifWanted / doubted / felt-about actionsJe veux que tu viennes. — I want you to come.

The imperative is nearly free: it’s the present tense minus the subject (Regarde ! Attendez ! Allons-y ! — tu-form -er verbs drop the s). The conditional is covered with the tenses. That leaves the famous one.

When the subjunctive is required

The subjunctive appears in the second half of a sentence, after que, when the first half pushes the action out of plain reality. The formula is:

[trigger] + que + [subject] + [subjunctive verb]

The triggers fall into a few families:

1. Wanting and requiring

Je veux que tu viennes. · Il faut que je parte.
I want you to come. · I have to leave. (vouloir que, il faut que, exiger que…)

2. Emotions

Je suis content que tu sois là. · J’ai peur qu’il soit trop tard.
I’m glad you’re here. · I’m afraid it’s too late. (être content/triste/désolé que, avoir peur que…)

3. Doubt and denial

Je ne pense pas qu’il ait raison. · Je doute que ce soit vrai.
I don’t think he’s right. · I doubt it’s true.
⚠️ The pense-que trap Je pense que (affirmative) takes the ordinary indicative: Je pense qu’il a raison. Only the negative/question versions (je ne pense pas que…) trigger the subjunctive — because that’s where the doubt is.

4. Certain conjunctions

avant qu’il parte · bien que ce soit difficile · pour que tu comprennes · à moins qu’il pleuve
before he leaves · although it’s difficult · so that you understand · unless it rains
💡 The one-second test No que? Then (almost always) no subjunctive. Same subject in both halves? French prefers an infinitive anyway (je veux partir, not je veux que je parte). The subjunctive only turns up when person A has feelings about person B doing something.

How to form it

Take the ils/elles form of the present, drop -ent, add -e, -es, -e, -ions, -iez, -ent:

ils finissent → que je finisse · ils partent → que je parte · ils prennent → que je prenne
For -er verbs the result sounds identical to the present: que je parle = je parle. You already say a lot of correct subjunctive without noticing.

🪄 Learn chunks, not tables In practice a few frozen phrases cover most real-life subjunctive: il faut que j’y aille (I’ve got to go — said constantly), que ce soit… ou… (whether it’s… or…), pourvu que ça marche (let’s hope it works), c’est dommage que tu ne puisses pas venir (shame you can’t come). Memorise these as whole sentences and you’ll sound fluent in exactly the places it counts.

How to avoid the subjunctive (honest edition)

Native speakers structure sentences to dodge the subjunctive all the time. These are real, natural rephrasings — not cheating:

1. Swap “il faut que” for an infinitive

Il faut que je parte → Il faut partir · Je dois partir
I have to leave. The lever verbs from Speaking Tricks take an infinitive, never the subjunctive.

2. Use “espérer” instead of “vouloir que”

Je veux qu’il vienne → J’espère qu’il viendra
I hope he’ll come. Espérer que takes the plain indicative — one of the few “feeling” verbs that does.

3. “avant de” + infinitive when subjects match

avant que je parte → avant de partir
before leaving. Same subject on both sides? The infinitive version is actually preferred.

4. State opinions positively

Je ne pense pas que ce soit vrai → Je pense que c’est faux · À mon avis, c’est faux
I think it’s false / In my opinion it’s false. Affirmative penser que and opinion-openers (à mon avis, pour moi, d’après moi) take no subjunctive at all.

5. “peut-être que” for uncertainty

Il est possible qu’il soit en retard → Peut-être qu’il est en retard
Maybe he’s late. Peut-être que + indicative expresses the same doubt, subjunctive-free.

6. Split the sentence

Je suis content que tu sois là → Tu es là ? Super, je suis content !
Two simple sentences instead of one complex one. Sounds more conversational anyway.
💡 Perspective Even if you never master the subjunctive, using the indicative where the subjunctive belongs is a small error that never blocks understanding — French kids get it wrong for years. Learn the frozen chunks, use the escapes above, and let the rest come with exposure.
