Speaking Tricks

How to communicate in French before your French is good. These are force multipliers: each one turns a small amount of grammar into a large amount of usable language.

The lever verbs — say anything with the infinitive

This is the Michel Thomas insight, and it’s the highest-value trick in French. Conjugating verbs is hard. But if you start your sentence with one of a handful of “lever” verbs, the verb that carries your actual meaning stays in the infinitive — the dictionary form. No conjugation needed.

Learn these levers in the je / tu / vous forms and you can express wants, plans, abilities, obligations and recent events for every verb in the language:

LeverMeaningExample
je vaisI’m going to…Je vais réfléchir. — I’m going to think about it.
je veuxI want to…Je veux comprendre. — I want to understand.
je voudrais / j’aimeraisI’d like to…Je voudrais réserver une table. — I’d like to book a table.
je peuxI can…Je peux vous aider ? — Can I help you?
je doisI have to…Je dois partir. — I have to leave.
il fautit’s necessary to… / you have to…Il faut réserver à l’avance. — You have to book ahead.
je viens deI just…Je viens de manger. — I just ate.
je suis en train deI’m in the middle of…Je suis en train de travailler. — I’m working (right now).
j’ai besoin deI need to…J’ai besoin de dormir. — I need to sleep.
j’ai envie deI feel like…J’ai envie de sortir. — I feel like going out.
j’essaie deI’m trying to…J’essaie d’apprendre le français. — I’m trying to learn French.
🪄 Why this works so well Ten conjugated forms buy you thousands of sentences, because the interesting verb never changes: je vais partir / je dois partir / je viens de partir / j’ai envie de partir. You choose the time and the mood with the lever; the meaning-verb just sits there in dictionary form. When you learn a new verb, you can use it in twenty ways immediately — without knowing how to conjugate it at all.
💡 Notice: il faut avoids even more Il faut never changes — there’s no “je faux”! Il faut partir = we/you/one must leave. It’s impersonal, so it also lets you dodge the subjunctive (see Moods) and dodge choosing a subject at all.

Time travel with two verbs

Combine two levers — aller (future) and venir de (recent past) — with the plain present, and you can place any action in time without conjugating it:

Je viens de manger. → Je suis en train de manger. → Je vais manger.
I just ate. → I’m eating. → I’m going to eat. — Past, present, future. Manger never changed.

Add the passé composé of just avoir and être and you cover nearly all everyday conversation. Real fluency comes later; communication can start today.

Questions the lazy (i.e. native) way

Textbooks teach inversion (Voulez-vous… ?) and est-ce que. Spoken French mostly does neither — it just raises the pitch at the end:

Tu viens ? · C’est loin ? · On y va ? · Tu as vu ça ?
Statement word order + rising intonation = question. This is how people actually talk.

For “wh-” questions, spoken French pushes the question word to the end:

Tu pars quand ? · C’est où ? · Ça coûte combien ? · Tu fais quoi ?
When do you leave? · Where is it? · How much is it? · What are you doing?

The “c’est” machine

C’est (“it is”) plus an adjective handles an enormous share of opinions, and it never conjugates for person:

C’est bon · c’est vrai · c’est possible · c’est trop cher · c’est pas grave · c’était super
It’s good · true · possible · too expensive · no big deal · it was great.

Learn exactly three forms — c’est (is), c’était (was), ça va être (is going to be) — and you can comment on anything in any tense. Related power tools: il y a (there is/are), ça as an all-purpose “it/that” (ça marche, ça dépend, ça m’intéresse).

When you’re stuck mid-sentence

  • Downgrade the noun. Don’t know “corkscrew”? Say le truc pour ouvrir le vin — “the thing for opening wine”. Un truc pour + infinitive names almost any object.
  • Downgrade the verb. Faire covers a huge range: faire du sport, faire les courses, faire attention. When the exact verb won’t come, try faire.
  • Say you’re searching. Comment dire… / Comment on dit… ? (“how do you say…”) is infinitely better than silence, and invites help.
  • Ask for the word. C’est quoi le mot pour… ? — “What’s the word for…?” French people generally enjoy answering this.
  • Recruit your listener. Vous voyez ce que je veux dire ? — “You see what I mean?” Half-communicated ideas get completed by the other person all the time.
  • Slow the game down. Attendez… / Alors… / Comment dire… are legitimate stalling moves. Native speakers stall constantly; it sounds more fluent than stopping dead.
🪄 The 80% sentence A sentence that’s 80% right and delivered with confidence beats a perfect sentence you never finish building. French people reconstruct your meaning from context, exactly as you do when a French person speaks imperfect English. Errors in gender, agreement and tense almost never block understanding — hesitation does.

Cheap polish — sound better than you are

  • Open with a connector. Starting sentences with Alors… / Bon… / En fait… / Du coup… instantly sounds native (see Spoken French).
  • Use on, not nous. On va au cinéma — easier to conjugate and more natural.
  • Drop the ne. Je sais pas, c’est pas possible. One less word to say, and it’s what everyone says.
  • Make it je voudrais, not je veux, when asking for things. One letter of conditional buys a lot of politeness.
  • Learn chunks, not words. Ça a l’air bien (that looks good), ça vaut le coup (it’s worth it), j’ai hâte (I can’t wait) — memorise these as single units and deploy them whole.
  • Echo-answer. Someone asks C’était bien ? — answer C’était super. Reusing the shape of the question halves the grammar you need to generate.
