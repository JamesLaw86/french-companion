Pronouns: y, en & Friends

The little words — y, en, le, la, lui, leur — are what make French sound French, and they’re a classic “keep having to look it up” topic. Here’s the whole system on one page.

Why these words exist

French hates repeating nouns. Where English happily says “Have you been to Paris? Yes, I’ve been to Paris”, French replaces the second mention with a pronoun — and unlike English “it/there”, the pronoun jumps in front of the verb:

— Tu es allé à Paris ? — Oui, j’y suis allé.
— Have you been to Paris? — Yes, I’ve been (there).

That “small word before the verb” habit is 90% of what makes these hard to hear at first — and what makes you sound genuinely French once you use them.

y — “there / about it”

Replaces: à + a place or thing (never a person).

Tu vas au bureau ? — Oui, j’y vais maintenant.
Are you going to the office? — Yes, I’m going (there) now.
Tu penses à ton examen ? — Oui, j’y pense tout le temps.
Are you thinking about your exam? — Yes, I think about it all the time. (penser à → y)

Frozen phrases you already know — y is hiding in plain sight:

Il y a · On y va ! · Allons-y · Vas-y · Ça y est · Je n’y peux rien
There is · Let’s go! · Let’s go · Go ahead · That’s it/done · There’s nothing I can do about it

en — “of it / of them / some”

Replaces: de + a thing, and quantities. If the noun came with du, de la, des, de or a number, en is its replacement.

Tu veux du café ? — Oui, j’en veux bien.
Do you want some coffee? — Yes, I’d like some.
Tu as des frères ? — J’en ai deux.
Do you have brothers? — I have two (of them). English can drop “of them”; French can’t drop the en.
Tu as besoin d’aide ? — Non, je n’en ai pas besoin.
Do you need help? — No, I don’t need it. (avoir besoin de → en)

Everyday idioms built on en — worth learning as whole chunks:

J’en ai marre · Je m’en fiche · Ne t’en fais pas · Je m’en vais · Qu’est-ce que tu en penses ? · Il en reste ?
I’m fed up · I don’t care · Don’t worry · I’m off · What do you think (of it)? · Is there any left?
🪄 Quick rule of thumb à-flavoured things → y. de-flavoured things and amounts → en. When the verb takes à or de (penser à, avoir besoin de), the pronoun is already chosen for you.

Direct objects: le, la, les — “him, her, it, them”

For the thing directly receiving the action. Same shapes as “the”, and le/la shrink to l’ before a vowel:

Tu as vu le film ? — Oui, je l’ai vu hier.
Did you see the film? — Yes, I saw it yesterday.
Tu prends les clés ? — Oui, je les prends.
Are you taking the keys? — Yes, I’m taking them.
Je te vois. · Il nous connaît. · Je vous écoute.
I see you. · He knows us. · I’m listening (to you). — me/te/nous/vous work for people the same way.

Indirect objects: lui, leur — “to him/her, to them”

For a person after à: parler à, téléphoner à, donner à, dire à, demander à, répondre à… One word covers both genders:

Tu as parlé à Marie ? — Oui, je lui ai parlé ce matin.
Did you speak to Marie? — Yes, I spoke to her this morning.
Je vais leur envoyer un message.
I’m going to send them a message.
⚠️ y vs lui à + thing/place → y, but à + person → lui/leur: Je pense à mon travail → J’y pense, but Je téléphone à ma mère → Je lui téléphone.

Il me le donne. · Je le lui ai dit. · Il y en a deux. · Je t’en parle demain.
He gives it to me. · I told (it to) him. · There are two (of them). · I’ll tell you about it tomorrow.
💡 Don’t memorise the grid — memorise sentences In real speech only a few combinations are common. Learn je te le dis (I’m telling you), il y en a (there are some), je le lui ai donné (I gave it to him) as chunks and the grid takes care of itself. Also: y and en always come last — “y en” in that order, as in il y en a.

Where they go in the sentence

  • Simple tense: right before the verb — Je le vois.
  • Compound tense: before the auxiliary — Je l’ai vu. (Never between avoir and the participle.)
  • With a lever verb + infinitive: before the infinitive — Je vais le faire. Je peux t’aider. Je viens de lui parler. Another win for the lever-verb habit: the pronoun placement is actually easier than in a conjugated sentence.
  • Negation wraps the lot: Je ne le lui ai pas dit — though in speech the ne vanishes anyway: Je le lui ai pas dit.
  • Positive commands flip it: pronoun after the verb with a hyphen — Regarde-le ! Donne-moi ça ! Vas-y !

moi, toi, lui, elle… — the stressed pronouns

After prepositions, after c’est, and for emphasis, French uses a separate set: moi, toi, lui, elle, nous, vous, eux, elles:

C’est moi. · Viens avec nous. · Chez eux. · Moi, je pense que non.
It’s me. · Come with us. · At their place. · Me, I think not. (that “Moi, je…” opener is extremely French)

The escape hatch

Mid-sentence and can’t compute the pronoun in time? Just repeat the noun. Tu as vu le film ? — Oui, j’ai vu le film. Slightly clunky, completely clear, and far better than stalling. Pronouns are polish for your output — but train your ears on them hard, because natives compress them relentlessly: je le lui ai dit comes out as roughly “j’lui ai dit”.
